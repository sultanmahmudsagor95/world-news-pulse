US Flights Slowly Recover After Rare Nationwide Ground Stop Grounded Thousands

16 November 2026

Originally published on World News Pulse — read it on the main blog.

Monday, 16 November 2026 — United States air travel is slowly returning to normal on Monday after a rare nationwide ground stop on Friday grounded departures across the country and left tens of thousands of passengers stranded over the weekend.

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) ordered all aircraft scheduled to depart the United States to stay on the ground at about 3:30 p.m. Eastern Time on Friday, 13 November, after a failure in the communications system used by air traffic controllers and pilots. More than 3,500 aircraft were already in the air when the order was issued, and many were diverted or continued to their destinations. The ground stop was lifted at about 8:15 p.m. Eastern Time, after roughly four and a half hours, but its effects rippled through airline schedules for days.

According to figures from the flight-tracking service FlightAware, more than 9,100 flights were cancelled on Friday and about 8,500 were delayed. Airlines cancelled more than 6,000 further flights on Saturday, and around 2,000 more on Sunday, as aircraft and crews ended up in the wrong places and needed time to be repositioned. The Associated Press described the disruption as the longest nationwide halt to US air travel since the attacks of 11 September 2001.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the trouble began during work to replace ageing copper wiring with fibre-optic cable at a remote FAA facility near El Dorado, Arkansas. A computer router installed as part of the upgrade, carried out by contractor L3Harris Technologies, began sending faulty signals and flooded the network with about 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, overloading other equipment. The FAA said the surge destabilised 17 of its 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers, which manage high-altitude traffic, and affected some radar feeds.

Investigators have also examined equipment at a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, that may have contributed to the overload. The two routers involved have been removed and sent to an L3Harris facility for testing. Duffy said the fault lies either with the router itself or with how it was installed, and that it was still unclear which. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford apologised to affected passengers and said the decision to stop flights was taken as a safety precaution.

Officials have said there is no evidence of a cyberattack or other malicious activity. The FBI remains part of the investigation, and Duffy told reporters there was no indication of foreign involvement. The cause has not yet been finally established.

The disruption hit some of the country's busiest airports hardest. Dallas Fort-Worth and Chicago O'Hare were among the worst affected, and American Airlines and SkyWest recorded the highest numbers of cancellations, according to FlightAware data. Flights departing from the United Kingdom and Europe were also cancelled as the effects spread through international schedules. Passengers described long waits at airports, with some remaining on aircraft for hours after landing while airlines worked to rebook them or return their luggage.

President Donald Trump, who had been silent on the disruption until Sunday, blamed the administration of former President Joe Biden for the state of federal aviation infrastructure and urged Congress to approve more money to modernise the air-traffic control system. Duffy said the administration would send Congress an emergency funding request for $50 billion to replace ageing equipment, noting that $12.5 billion approved last year was only a first step.

By Monday, airlines including Delta and Alaska Air said they expected a more normal service or sharply reduced cancellations, and FlightAware data showed cancellations easing. Duffy said on Sunday that flights should be "back to normal" by Monday, although he warned that full recovery would take time because aircraft and crews were still out of position.

The incident has renewed scrutiny of the age of America's air-traffic control infrastructure, much of which still relies on decades-old technology. Officials say modernising it while keeping the current system running safely is the central challenge — one that Friday's failure has now made impossible to ignore.
