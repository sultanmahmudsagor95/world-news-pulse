Trump Says He Would Strike Iran Again With 'No Hesitation'

13 November 2026

Originally published on World News Pulse — read it on the main blog.

US President Donald Trump speaks at the White House. (Photo: The White House)

US President Donald Trump said on Thursday, 12 November 2026, that he would not hesitate to launch new military strikes against Iran if he judged it necessary, suggesting that further action could begin immediately.

Asked in an interview with Hugh Hewitt on the Salem News Channel whether he would hesitate to use military force to stop Iran from threatening United States allies and shipping, Trump replied: "No hesitation."

"If I saw things going the other way, I would do that. And it's possible I would do it right now," he added, according to Al Jazeera and the Times of Israel, which both reported the exchange from the interview.

The remarks came in the same interview in which Trump also made sweeping claims about the state of the conflict. He said the United States had complete control over the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which much of the world's oil is shipped, and that Iran's military capabilities had been largely destroyed. He also claimed that Iran's economy had been destroyed, that inflation in the country had reached 301 percent, and that the Iranian government was on the brink of collapse. Those are Trump's own claims. The news reports carrying them did not provide independent verification, and this article does not present them as established facts.

Trump also said he would consider it a defeat if Iran's government were still in power when his term ends. Asked about that possibility in the same interview, he replied: "Yes, that would be a defeat… Absolutely," according to the Times of Israel.

The warning followed a different message from Trump earlier the same day. In a post on Truth Social on Thursday morning, he wrote that the United States did not need a deal with Iran, and he said he was directing Vice President JD Vance, who had been leading negotiations with Tehran, to focus instead on alleged corruption and crime in Democratic-run American cities. Vance had led talks that produced a memorandum in June, which broke down within weeks, according to Reuters.

In a separate interview on Thursday with broadcaster Stephen A. Smith, Trump said he was holding off further large-scale strikes at the request of Gulf states. "Saudi Arabia has asked me to please just let it be the way it is now for a while," he said, naming Qatar, Kuwait, the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain as countries that had made the same request. Asked whether he would have launched further attacks without those requests, he replied: "Yes, I would." He also claimed he could destroy Iran's energy infrastructure, bridges and manufacturing within three days, a claim reported without independent verification.

The mixed signals stood in contrast to Trump's comments earlier in the week. He told CBS's "60 Minutes" on Sunday that he was "not looking" to escalate the war, and on Monday he told reporters in the White House briefing room: "At this point, it won't be necessary, taking the military action." Days earlier, he had described renewed attacks as "quite unlikely," according to Al Jazeera. CNN, reporting on Thursday's interviews, said Trump had offered mixed messages on his approach to the war with Iran.

Iran responded on Thursday with a warning of its own. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baqaei said countries that provided military or logistical assistance to the United States for operations against Tehran would be viewed as committing a hostile act and would bear responsibility for aggression against Iran, invoking international law and the United Nations Charter.

On Friday, 13 November, Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed it had targeted and destroyed an American early-warning radar in Bahrain, in response to what it described as an attack on a telecommunications tower in Sirik, on Iran's coast near the Strait of Hormuz. Neither the United States military nor Bahrain's government had commented by Friday morning, and no independent confirmation of the claim had been reported. It is presented here only as a claim by the Revolutionary Guard Corps, not as a confirmed strike.

Trump did not announce a new strike on Thursday, and he gave no date for any further action. His remarks left open both possibilities: renewed military force if he judged that conditions had changed, and a pause while Gulf allies asked him to wait. What happens next is likely to depend on whether those diplomatic efforts hold, and on whether either side takes a step that changes the current pause.
